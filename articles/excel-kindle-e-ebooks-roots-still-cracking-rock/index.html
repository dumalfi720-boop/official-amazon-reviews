Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

What is a roots still and how does it work? ▾
A roots still is a basic distillation apparatus that heats a fermented liquid in a boiler, captures the vapor in a condenser, and collects the condensed spirit in a receiving vessel. The process relies on the different boiling points of alcohol and water to separate them. For beginners, start with a simple pot‑still configuration because it has fewer moving parts and is easier to control. Always verify that your boiler and condenser are made of food‑grade stainless steel or copper before the first run.
How do I set up a roots still for the first time? ▾
Begin by cleaning all components with a food‑safe sanitizer and rinsing thoroughly. Assemble the boiler, connect the condenser with tight‑fitting gaskets, and attach a collection jar at the condenser outlet. Fill the boiler with your wash, leaving at least 20 % headspace to prevent boil‑over. Turn on the heat gradually, monitor the temperature with a calibrated probe, and adjust the cooling water flow so the condenser stays cool but not frozen. Record each setting in a log for future reference.
Why should I choose a roots still over other distillation methods? ▾
A roots still offers a straightforward, low‑cost entry point because it requires only a boiler, condenser, and collector without complex reflux plates or automated controls. This simplicity makes it beginner‑friendly and reduces the chance of mechanical failure. It also lets you learn the fundamentals of vapor‑liquid equilibrium hands‑on before investing in more advanced equipment. If your goal is to produce small batches of high‑quality spirit for personal use, a roots still is often the most practical choice.
When is the best time to run a distillation batch? ▾
Run your batch when you have a stable ambient temperature and enough uninterrupted time — typically 4 to 6 hours — to monitor the process from start to finish. Avoid running during extreme heat or cold, as ambient conditions affect condenser efficiency. Schedule the run after you have verified all safety checks, gathered cleaning supplies, and prepared a fire‑extinguishing method nearby. Consistency in timing helps you compare results across batches.
What safety equipment do I need for home distillation? ▾
Essential safety gear includes a calibrated pressure‑relief valve on the boiler, a reliable thermometer with a probe rated for high heat, heat‑resistant gloves, and safety goggles. Keep a Class B fire extinguisher within arm’s reach and install a carbon‑monoxide detector in the workspace. Use a grounded, GFCI‑protected outlet for any electric heating element. Never leave the still unattended while it is heating.
How can I control temperature accurately during a run? ▾
Install a digital thermometer with a probe that sits directly in the vapor path, not just the liquid, to capture the true boiling temperature. Adjust the heat source in small increments — 5 % power changes — and wait 2–3 minutes for the system to stabilize before making another adjustment. Use a PID controller if you have an electric heater; it automates fine‑tuning and reduces temperature swings. Log the temperature every 5 minutes for later analysis.
What are the legal considerations for operating a still at home? ▾
In the United States, federal law requires a distilled spirits permit for any production of beverage alcohol, even for personal use, and many states impose additional licensing or outright bans. Check the Alcohol and Tobacco Tax and Trade Bureau (TTB) website and your state’s alcohol control board before purchasing equipment. Keep records of all runs, including volumes and dates, in case of inspection. Non‑compliance can result in heavy fines and equipment seizure.
How do I clean and maintain my equipment after each use? ▾
After the run, disassemble the still while it is still warm (but not hot) to loosen residues. Rinse each part with hot water, then soak in a solution of 1 % food‑grade citric acid for 30 minutes to dissolve mineral deposits. Scrub gently with a non‑abrasive brush, rinse thoroughly, and sanitize with a no‑rinse sanitizer approved for brewing. Dry completely before storage, and inspect gaskets and seals for wear before the next batch.
